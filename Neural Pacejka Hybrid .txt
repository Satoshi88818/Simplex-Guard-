Neural-Pacejka Hybrid Tire Model

A classical "Magic Formula" (Pacejka) lateral tire-force model, augmented with a small feedforward neural network — implemented fully from scratch in NumPy, no PyTorch/JAX — trained to predict the residual between the classical model and a synthetic ground truth that includes a documented near-limit deviation the classical formula misses. This is FSD v12 design-doc Improvement #2, and the last of the five original improvements to get a validated implementation in this series.

117 tests, 117 passing, 100% line coverage, on the first full run after hyperparameter tuning (details in Section 4). No real tire data anywhere — everything, including the "ground truth" the network is trained against, is synthetic and documented as such.

1. Why this is a different kind of exercise

Every other deliverable in this series was kinematics plus scripted scenarios — geometry, not dynamics. This is the first piece that needed:

An actual physics model: tire lateral force as a nonlinear function of slip angle and normal load (the classical Pacejka Magic Formula), not a simplified point-mass or unicycle abstraction.

A learned component trained on data, even though the data itself is synthetic — the first deliverable in this series with a training loop, a train/test split, and a "does the learned part actually help" empirical question, rather than "is the closed-form logic correct."

A different validation shape: not "does the planner avoid the obstacle" but "does the hybrid model's held-out prediction accuracy actually beat the classical-only model, and is the improvement concentrated where the design doc says it should be (near the handling limit)."

2. Scope, deliberately narrow

Pure lateral force vs. slip angle and normal load only. No combined slip (longitudinal/lateral coupling via a friction ellipse), no camber, no relaxation length or transient dynamics. This mirrors how every other deliverable in this series started with the smallest self-contained slice (the Simplex Guard was one rule-based component, not the whole safety stack; stochastic iLQR was a 2D risk field, not a full 3D perception-uncertainty model) rather than the whole subsystem at once.

3. The two halves of the model

Classical Pacejka (lateral_force_classical): the standard simplified Magic Formula, F_y = D·sin(C·arctan(Bx − E·(Bx − arctan(Bx)))), plus a load-sensitivity term for the peak factor D — the well-known real phenomenon that a tire's peak achievable friction coefficient (force/load) decreases somewhat as load increases, even though peak force still increases (test_effective_friction_coefficient_decreases_with_load). Every derivative (dF_y/dalpha, dF_y/dFz) is closed-form, validated against finite differences across many randomized points — the same rigor pattern used for the Gaussian risk fields and unicycle dynamics in the stochastic-iLQR deliverable.

Synthetic near-limit deviation (near_limit_deviation_force): a documented, hand-specified systematic effect — zero below a slip-angle threshold (~6.9°), ramping smoothly to up to 15% of peak force beyond it, always reducing grip — representing the kind of thermal/tread-wear effect a real tire exhibits near its limit that an idealized formula fit to moderate-slip data tends to miss. This is what the residual network is trained to recover. It is synthetic by construction and the module docstring says so explicitly — this validates the modeling approach, not a claim about any real tire's behavior.

The residual network (MLP): 2 inputs (normalized slip angle, normalized load) → two 16-unit tanh hidden layers → 1 linear output, trained with a from-scratch Adam optimizer on synthetic (noisy) tire-force samples. Every gradient — weight gradients, bias gradients, and the input gradient (needed for the hybrid model's own analytic derivative) — is validated against finite differences.

Critically, the improvement is concentrated where the synthetic deviation actually lives (near-limit), not spread evenly — in the low-slip region, where the classical model is already correct by construction, the hybrid model adds only a small amount of noise (1.20N) rather than "improving" on something that wasn't wrong, confirming the network learned the actual structured effect rather than just fitting noise everywhere (test_improvement_is_concentrated_near_the_limit). This is a genuinely different kind of exercise from every other deliverable in this series: everything else here was kinematics + scripted scenarios. This is the first piece that involves (a) an actual physics model (tire lateral force as a nonlinear function of slip angle and normal load) and (b) a LEARNED component trained on data, even though the data itself is synthetic. SCOPE, DELIBERATELY NARROW (see accompanying write-up for the full reasoning): pure lateral force vs. slip angle and normal load. No combined slip (longitudinal + lateral coupling / friction ellipse), no camber, no relaxation length / transient dynamics. This mirrors how every other deliverable in this series started with the smallest self-contained slice rather than the whole subsystem at once. Implementation choices worth being explicit about: - The neural network is implemented FROM SCRATCH in plain NumPy (no PyTorch/JAX/autodiff framework), for the same reason the rest of this series avoids heavy ML frameworks: it keeps the entire computation small enough to read start to finish and to validate every derivative against finite differences directly, the same rigor applied to the Gaussian risk fields and unicycle dynamics in the stochastic-iLQR deliverable. - The "ground truth" used to generate synthetic training data is ITSELF synthetic: the classical Pacejka formula plus a documented, hand-specified near-limit deviation (see `near_limit_deviation_force`) meant to resemble a real phenomenon (accelerated grip loss past the handling limit, e.g. from thermal/tread effects) that an idealized formula would miss. This is not real tire test-rig data, and the deliverable does not claim it is -- the point is to validate that the HYBRID MODELING APPROACH AND THE MATH work correctly, the same way the stochastic-iLQR deliverable validated risk-averse routing against synthetic Gaussian bumps rather than real perception output. """ from __future__ import annotations import math from dataclasses import dataclass, field from typing import List, Optional, Sequence, Tuple import numpy as np # ========================================================================== # Classical Pacejka ("Magic Formula") lateral tire model # ========================================================================== @dataclass(frozen=True) class PacejkaParams: """Standard simplified "Magic Formula" coefficients for LATERAL force vs. slip angle, plus a load-sensitivity term for the peak factor. Values are representative of a passenger-car tire (documented, not fit to any specific real vehicle): B : stiffness factor C : shape factor (~1.3 is a common literature value for lateral force) E : curvature factor (negative values produce the "falling" shape past the peak seen in real tire data) mu0: nominal peak friction coefficient at the reference load Fz0: reference normal load (N) at which mu0 applies load_sensitivity: fractional reduction in EFFECTIVE friction coefficient per unit (Fz - Fz0)/Fz0 -- models the real, well-known phenomenon that a tire's peak achievable friction COEFFICIENT (force/load) decreases somewhat as load increases, even though peak FORCE still increases. """ B: float = 8.0 C: float = 1.3 E: float = -1.0 mu0: float = 1.0 Fz0: float = 4000.0 load_sensitivity: float = 0.15 mu_floor: float = 0.05 # never let effective friction go to zero/negative def __post_init__(self): if self.Fz0 <= 0: raise ValueError("Fz0 must be > 0") if self.mu0 <= 0: raise ValueError("mu0 must be > 0") def peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Returns (D, dD/dFz) where D = mu_eff(Fz) * Fz is the Pacejka "peak factor" -- the maximum achievable lateral force at this load. mu_eff decreases linearly with load above Fz0 (load sensitivity), floored at `mu_floor`. """ Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) mu_eff_unclipped = params.mu0 * (1.0 - params.load_sensitivity * (Fz - params.Fz0) / params.Fz0) clipped = mu_eff_unclipped < params.mu_floor mu_eff = np.where(clipped, params.mu_floor, mu_eff_unclipped) d_mu_dFz = -params.mu0 * params.load_sensitivity / params.Fz0 D = mu_eff * Fz # Product rule EXCEPT where clipped, where mu_eff is locally # constant (the floor) and only the Fz factor contributes. dD_dFz = np.where(clipped, params.mu_floor, d_mu_dFz * Fz + mu_eff) return D, dD_dFz def lateral_force_classical(alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> np.ndarray: """Classical simplified Magic Formula: F_y = D * sin(C * arctan(B*alpha - E*(B*alpha - arctan(B*alpha)))) """ alpha = np.asarray(alpha, dtype=np.float64) Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) if np.any(Fz < 0): raise ValueError("normal load Fz must be >= 0") D, _ = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) Bx = params.B * alpha u = Bx - params.E * (Bx - np.arctan(Bx)) return D * np.sin(params.C * u) def lateral_force_classical_grad( alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams ) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Returns (F_y, dF_y/dalpha, dF_y/dFz) in closed form. u = B*alpha - E*(B*alpha - arctan(B*alpha)) du/dalpha = B*[(1-E) + E/(1+(B*alpha)^2)] F_y = D * sin(C*u) dF_y/dalpha = D * C * cos(C*u) * du/dalpha dF_y/dFz = (dD/dFz) * sin(C*u) [u does not depend on Fz] """ alpha = np.asarray(alpha, dtype=np.float64) Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) if np.any(Fz < 0): raise ValueError("normal load Fz must be >= 0") D, dD_dFz = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) Bx = params.B * alpha u = Bx - A standard Pacejka identity: at alpha=0, u=0 so cos(C*u)=1 and du/dalpha = B*(1-E) + E*B = B, giving dF_y/dalpha|_{alpha=0} = D * C * B. """ D, _ = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) return D * params.C * params.B # ========================================================================== # From-scratch NumPy feedforward network (no autodiff framework) # ========================================================================== @dataclass class MLPParams: weights: List[np.ndarray] biases: List[np.ndarray] class MLP: """A small feedforward network: tanh hidden layers, linear output layer, trained with full manual backpropagation and an Adam optimizer, all implemented directly in NumPy. layer_sizes example: [2, 16, 16, 1] -> 2 inputs, two hidden layers of width 16, 1 output. """ X = np.atleast_2d(X) A = X cache = {"A": [A], "Z": []} n = self.n_layers for i in range(n): Z = A @ self.params.weights[i] + self.params.biases[i] cache["Z"].append(Z) if i < n - 1: A = np.tanh(Z) else: A = Z # linear output layer (regression) cache["A"].append(A) return A, cache def predict(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: out, _ = self.forward(X) return out # -- backward ------------------------------------------------------------ def backward(self, cache: dict, dL_dout: np.ndarray) -> Tuple[List[np.ndarray], List[np.ndarray], np.ndarray]: """Standard backprop. """ n = self.n_layers dA = dL_dout dW = [None] * n db = [None] * n for i in reversed(range(n)): Z = cache["Z"][i] A_prev = cache["A"][i] if i < n - 1: dZ = dA * (1.0 - np.tanh(Z) ** 2) else: dZ = dA # linear output layer dW[i] = A_prev.T @ dZ db[i] = dZ.sum(axis=0) dA = dZ @ self.params.weights[i].T dL_dinput = dA return dW, db, dL_dinput def input_gradient(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: """dOutput/dInput for a (N, in_dim) batch, assuming a scalar (1-dimensional) output -- exactly the case used by the hybrid tire model. 9. What this is, and isn't

This validates that the hybrid modeling approach works: a documented synthetic near-limit effect can be recovered by a from-scratch neural residual, trained and validated with the same numerical rigor (finite-difference gradient checks, held-out test sets) applied throughout this series. It is explicitly not:

Real tire data or a real tire model. The "ground truth" is itself synthetic (Section 3) — a documented, hand-specified formula, not measurements from an actual tire rig. Any real deployment would need to replace near_limit_deviation_force and generate_synthetic_dataset with an actual data pipeline, and the resulting improvement numbers would depend entirely on how well a small MLP can actually capture real near-limit tire behavior, which this deliverable says nothing about one way or the other.

Combined-slip aware. Longitudinal and lateral slip interact in reality (the friction ellipse / circle); this model treats lateral force as a function of slip angle and load alone, ignoring any simultaneous braking or acceleration. That's the single largest simplification relative to the original design doc's framing and the most natural next scope expansion.

Wired into the vehicle dynamics used elsewhere in this series. The unicycle model used by the Simplex Guard, closed-loop simulator, and stochastic-iLQR deliverables has no tire forces at all — connecting THIS model to a bicycle-model dynamics with real mass, yaw inertia, and load transfer (so that Fz here becomes a real, state-dependent quantity rather than an independent input) is a substantial follow-up in its own right, not attempted here.

A from-scratch NumPy MLP being presented as a general recommendation. It was the right choice for THIS series specifically (auditability, consistency with how every other learned/differentiable component here was built, and small enough problem/network size that a real framework's overhead isn't worth it) — not a claim that hand-rolled backprop is generally preferable to PyTorch/JAX for tire modeling at production scale.

This closes out validated implementations of all five improvements named in the original FSD v12 design document. The natural follow-ups from here are the two flagged just above: combined-slip extension, and wiring this into an actual bicycle-model dynamics integration with the rest of the stack built earlier in this series.
