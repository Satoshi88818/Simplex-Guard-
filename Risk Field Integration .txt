2. The second headline finding: the symmetric saddle reappears

The original stochastic-iLQR deliverable found that a perfectly symmetric obstacle exactly on the straight-line path creates a true local-optimization saddle — going left or right costs identically, so the optimizer has no gradient to break the tie on its own. The multi-hypothesis-iLQR deliverable fixed this for the unicycle with a small set of symmetry-breaking initial guesses.

This deliverable checked whether the saddle is specific to the unicycle's simpler dynamics, or a property of the cost landscape itself — and it's the latter. With a perfectly symmetric obstacle and a symmetric initial guess, the bicycle+combined-slip optimizer gets stuck on the centerline exactly the same way:

No symmetry-breaking nudge: min_distance = 0.00 (stuck on the saddle) With a small nudge: min_distance = 4.24 (resolves cleanly) 

This wasn't something this deliverable set out to test — it fell out of trying to build the headline scenario correctly (an early attempt at the detour scenario used a symmetric obstacle placement and reproduced the exact same stuck behavior before the placement was deliberately moved off-center for the primary comparison). It's included here as a genuine, if secondary, finding: TestSymmetricSaddleUnderBicycleDynamics locks in both directions.

3. Confirming (again) that the solver needed no changes

RiskAwareBicycleCostConfig is the only new code in this deliverable — a cost object implementing the same four-method protocol (stage_cost, terminal_cost, stage_derivatives, terminal_derivatives) that GenericILQRSolver has always expected, confirmed by inspection (the solver only ever calls these methods on its cost argument — no isinstance checks anywhere) and now confirmed again by a direct test (test_solver_accepts_risk_aware_cost_without_modification). This is the fourth deliverable in a row — following the bicycle-dynamics and combined-slip deliverables, and the original design intent behind generalizing the solver in the first place — that adds real new capability (here: risk-awareness on top of real vehicle dynamics) without touching the optimizer itself.

4. What "converged=True" needed, and an honest limitation found while tuning

Getting the headline table to converge cleanly took real tuning, documented here rather than only showing the final numbers (same practice as the neural-Pacejka deliverable's own training-tuning narrative):

The first parameter set tried did not converge cleanly. With a short horizon (60–80 steps) and a modest terminal position weight, several configurations either failed the strict predicted-improvement convergence check even after 200+ iterations, or converged to a trajectory that never fully returned to lane center by the end of the horizon (one case ended 2.5m off-center).

The fix was a longer horizon (120 steps), a heavier terminal position weight (200, up from 80), and a real terminal heading weight (100, previously absent) — giving the optimizer enough time and enough incentive to complete the detour-and-return maneuver, not just the detour.

Even after tuning, the lowest-speed, highest-risk-weight case still needed substantially more iterations to converge (437, versus 45–121 for the other eight configurations) and still ends with the largest residual offset in the table. This is a genuine, real property of the problem, not a bug: at low speed, the same obstacle (placed at a fixed fraction of the horizon's travel distance) is proportionally closer, leaving less remaining distance to complete the return maneuver — a finite-horizon planning effect that has nothing to do with tire grip specifically, but is worth knowing about before reading too much into any single number in the table.

""" step_fn = lambda s, c, dt_: combined_slip_bicycle_step(s, c, tire_model, pacejka_params, vp, dt_) jac_fn = lambda s, c, dt_: combined_slip_bicycle_jacobians(s, c, tire_model, pacejka_params, vp, dt_) return GenericILQRSolver( step_fn, jac_fn, cost, dt=dt, state_dim=STATE_DIM, control_dim=CONTROL_DIM, max_iters=max_iters, max_control=max_control, )

""" stochastic_ilqr.py -------------------- A from-scratch iLQR (iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator) trajectory optimizer for a kinematic unicycle robot, augmented with a STOCHASTIC RISK TERM that turns "epistemic uncertainty x occupancy" into a real-time-tractable, differentiable addition to the running cost -- this is Improvement #1 from the FSD v12 design document, implemented and validated end to end. Why this is the interesting piece to build -------------------------------------------- A standard MPC/iLQR cost only knows about a *point estimate* of where obstacles are (occupancy). It has no way to be more cautious in a region the perception system is unsure about versus a region it is confident is clear. The fix used here: maintain two scalar fields over the workspace -- occupancy(p) in [0, 1] : how likely something occupies point p uncertainty(p) in [0, 1] : how unsure the perception system is about that likelihood at point p and add a risk cost term l_risk(p) = risk_weight * uncertainty(p) * occupancy(p) to the running cost at every timestep along the trajectory. A region with high occupancy but LOW uncertainty (a confidently-detected wall) is treated as a normal hard-ish cost the planner will route around anyway. A region with moderate occupancy AND high uncertainty (e.g. "might be a pedestrian behind that parked van, sensor isn't sure") now carries extra cost specifically because it's unsure -- pushing the planner toward a route with a clearer, more confidently-assessed margin, even when the point-estimate occupancy alone wouldn't have forced a detour. Both fields are represented as sums of smooth Gaussian "bumps," which makes the risk term's gradient and Hessian available in closed form (no autodiff framework, no finite differences) -- this directly implements the design doc's stated refinement of replacing finite-difference Jacobians with analytic ones, and the analytic Jacobians for the *dynamics* are implemented the same way, for the same reason: finite-differencing inside a real-time control loop is expensive and noisy; closed-form derivatives are cheap and exact. Everything below is validated against finite differences in the test suite as an explicit correctness check, not just asserted. Scope and honesty about limitations -------------------------------------- - The dynamics model is a kinematic unicycle (state = x, y, heading; control = speed, turn-rate), not a full vehicle dynamics model with slip, tire forces, or actuator lag. - The risk field is a synthetic sum of Gaussian bumps, not a real perception system's uncertainty/occupancy output. The point of this exercise is to validate that the MATH and the OPTIMIZER correctly turn "uncertainty x occupancy" into risk-averse routing -- not to claim this is a deployable perception interface. - Obstacle avoidance here is soft (cost-based), not the hard constraint enforcement a real system would layer on top (that's what the Simplex Guard, built previously, is for). """ from __future__ import annotations import math from dataclasses import dataclass, field from typing import Callable, List, Optional, Sequence, Tuple import numpy as np # ========================================================================== # Scalar fields (uncertainty / occupancy), with analytic derivatives # ========================================================================== @dataclass(frozen=True) class GaussianBump: """An isotropic Gaussian bump in 2D: f(p) = amplitude * exp(-||p - center||^2 / (2 * sigma^2)) Closed-form gradient and Hessian (used directly by iLQR's cost quadratic expansion -- no autodiff, no finite differences): grad f(p) = f(p) * (-(p - center) / sigma^2) Hess f(p) = f(p) * ( outer(d, d) / sigma^4 - I / sigma^2 ), d = p - center """ center: Tuple[float, float] amplitude: float sigma: float def __post_init__(self): if self.sigma <= 0: raise ValueError("sigma must be > 0") def value(self, p: np.ndarray) -> float: d = p - np.asarray(self.center) return float(self.amplitude * math.exp(-float(d @ d) / (2.0 * self.sigma ** 2))) def gradient(self, p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: d = p - np.asarray(self.center) f = self.value(p) return f * (-d / self.sigma ** 2) def hessian(self, p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: d = p - np.asarray(self.center) f = self.value(p) outer = np.outer(d, d) return f * (outer / self.sigma ** 4 - np.eye(2) / self.sigma ** 2) @dataclass class ScalarField: """A sum of GaussianBumps, clipped to [0, 1] in VALUE ONLY for interpretation purposes (occupancy/uncertainty are probabilities). In practice the bump amplitudes are chosen so the sum stays within [0, 1] almost everywhere for the scenarios used here; this is documented and tested explicitly (see `test_field_values_stay_in_unit_range_for_test_scenarios`). """ bumps: List[GaussianBump] = field(default_factory=list) def value(self, p: np.ndarray) -> float: return float(sum(b.value(p) for b in self.bumps)) def gradient(self, p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: if not self.bumps: return np.zeros(2) return sum(b.gradient(p) for b in self.bumps) def hessian(self, p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: if not self.bumps: return np.zeros((2, 2)) return sum(b.hessian(p) for b in self.bumps) @dataclass class RiskField: """risk(p) = uncertainty(p) * occupancy(p), with exact gradient and Hessian via the product rule: h = u * o grad h = u * grad_o + o * grad_u Hess h = u * Hess_o + o * Hess_u + outer(grad_u, grad_o) + outer(grad_o, grad_u) """ uncertainty: ScalarField occupancy: ScalarField def value(self, p: np.ndarray) -> float: return self.uncertainty.value(p) * self.occupancy.value(p) def gradient(self, p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: u = self.uncertainty.value(p) o = self.occupancy.value(p) gu = self.uncertainty.gradient(p) go = self.occupancy.gradient(p) return u * go + o * gu def hessian(self, p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: u = self.uncertainty.value(p) o = self.occupancy.value(p) gu = self.uncertainty.gradient(p) go = self.occupancy.gradient(p) hu = self.uncertainty.hessian(p) ho = self.occupancy.hessian(p) return u * ho + o * hu + np.outer(gu, go) + np.outer(go, gu) # [x, y, heading] CONTROL_DIM = 2 # [speed, turn_rate] def unicycle_step(x: np.ndarray, u: np.ndarray, dt: float) -> np.ndarray: """x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t): x' = x + v * cos(heading) * dt y' = y + v * sin(heading) * dt heading' = heading + omega * dt """ px, py, heading = x v, omega = u return np.array( [ px + v * math.cos(heading) * dt, py + v * math.sin(heading) * dt, heading + omega * dt, ] ) def unicycle_jacobians(x: np.ndarray, u: np.ndarray, dt: float) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Analytic df/dx (A, 3x3) and df/du (B, 3x2) for the unicycle step. df/dx = [[1, 0, -v*sin(h)*dt], [0, 1, v*cos(h)*dt], [0, 0, 1 ]] df/du = [[cos(h)*dt, 0], [sin(h)*dt, 0], [0, dt]] """ _, _, heading = x v, _ = u A = np.array( [ [1.0, 0.0, -v * math.sin(heading) * dt], [0.0, 1.0, v * math.cos(heading) * dt], [0.0, 0.0, 1.0], ] ) B = np.array( [ [math.cos(heading) * dt, 0.0], [math.sin(heading) * dt, 0.0], [0.0, dt], ] ) return A, B # (2,) H = self.risk_field.hessian(p) # (2,2) l_x = l_x.copy() l_x[:2] += self.risk_weight * g l_xx = l_xx.copy() l_xx[:2, :2] += self.risk_weight * H # No u- or heading-dependence in the risk term, so l_u, # l_uu, l_ux get no contribution from it. return l_x, l_u, l_xx, l_uu, l_ux def terminal_derivatives(self, x: np.ndarray) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: dx = x - self.goal return self.Qf @ dx, self.Qf.copy() # -- backward pass ----------------------------------------------------- def _backward_pass( self, X: np.ndarray, U: np.ndarray, reg: float ) -> Optional[Tuple[List[np.ndarray], List[np.ndarray], float]]: """Returns (k_list, K_list, expected_improvement) or None if Q_uu could not be made positive-definite even at the current regularization (caller should increase reg and retry). `expected_improvement` is the standard DDP/iLQR predicted cost reduction sum_t [ -(Q_u^T k_t + 0.5 * k_t^T Q_uu k_t) ] -- computed directly from the backward pass, independent of whether a forward-pass line search actually finds an improving step. This is what lets the solver correctly recognize convergence when the cost is ALREADY at (or essentially at) its minimum: in that case Q_u ~= 0 everywhere, expected_improvement ~= 0, and no line-search step could ever reduce a cost that is already minimal -- that is a sign of convergence, not a sign that regularization needs to keep increasing forever. """ T = U.shape[0] Vx, Vxx = self.cost.terminal_derivatives(X[T]) k_list: List[np.ndarray] = [None] * T # type: ignore K_list: List[np.ndarray] = [None] * T # type: ignore expected_improvement = 0.0 for t in reversed(range(T)): x, u = X[t], U[t] A, B = unicycle_jacobians(x, u, self.dt) l_x, l_u, l_xx, l_uu, l_ux = self.cost.stage_derivatives(x, u) Q_x = l_x + A.T @ Vx Q_u = l_u + B.T @ Vx Q_xx = l_xx + A.T @ Vxx @ A Q_uu = l_uu + B.T @ Vxx @ B Q_ux = l_ux + B.T @ Vxx @ A Q_uu_reg = Q_uu + reg * np.eye(CONTROL_DIM) # Positive-definiteness check via Cholesky; signals the # caller to increase regularization and retry the WHOLE # backward pass if it fails partway through (standard # DDP/iLQR regularization scheme). try: L = np.linalg.cholesky(Q_uu_reg) except np.linalg.LinAlgError: return None Q_uu_inv = np.linalg.inv(Q_uu_reg) k = -Q_uu_inv @ Q_u K = -Q_uu_inv @ Q_ux expected_improvement += -(Q_u @ k + 0.5 * k @ Q_uu @ k) Vx = Q_x + K.T @ Q_uu @ k + K.T @ Q_u + Q_ux.T @ k Vxx = Q_xx + K.T @ Q_uu @ K + K.T @ Q_ux + Q_ux.T @ K Vxx = 0.5 * (Vxx + Vxx.T) # enforce symmetry against fp drift k_list[t] = k K_list[t] = K return k_list, K_list, expected_improvement # -- forward pass with line search ------------------------------------- def _forward_pass( self, X: np.ndarray, U: np.ndarray, k_list: List[np.ndarray], K_list: List[np.ndarray], current_cost: float, ) -> Optional[Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, float]]: T = U.shape[0] for alpha in self.line_search_alphas: X_new = np.zeros_like(X) U_new = np.zeros_like(U) X_new[0] = X[0] for t in range(T): dx = X_new[t] - X[t] u = U[t] + alpha * k_list[t] + K_list[t] @ dx u = self._clip_control(u) U_new[t] = u X_new[t + 1] = unicycle_step(X_new[t], u, self.dt) new_cost = self._total_cost(X_new, U_new) if np.isfinite(new_cost) and new_cost < current_cost: return X_new, U_new, new_cost return None # -- main solve loop ----------------------------------------------------- def solve(self, x0: np.ndarray, U_init: np.ndarray) -> ILQRResult: X = self._rollout(x0, U_init) U = U_init.copy() cost = self._total_cost(X, U) cost_history = [cost] reg = self.reg_init converged = False it = 0 for it in range(1, self.max_iters + 1): backward = None # Increase regularization until the backward pass succeeds # (Q_uu positive-definite at every timestep) or we give up. while backward is None: backward = self._backward_pass(X, U, reg) if backward is None: reg *= self.reg_factor if reg > self.reg_max: return ILQRResult(X, U, cost_history, False, it, reg) k_list, K_list, expected_improvement = backward # Convergence check BEFORE attempting a line search: if the # backward pass itself predicts negligible further # improvement is possible (e.g. we are already at, or # extremely close to, a local optimum -- Q_u ~= 0 # everywhere), no forward-pass step could ever succeed by # construction (there is nothing left to improve), and # that is a sign of CONVERGENCE, not a sign that # regularization needs to keep increasing. Without this # check, a problem that starts exactly at its optimum # (e.g. goal == start) would spin regularization up to # reg_max and incorrectly report failure. if expected_improvement < self.cost_tol: converged = True break result = self._forward_pass(X, U, k_list, K_list, cost) if result is None: # No line-search step improved cost: increase # regularization (be more conservative) and retry. reg *= self.reg_factor if reg > self.reg_max: break continue X_new, U_new, new_cost = result improvement = cost - new_cost X, U, cost = X_new, U_new, new_cost cost_history.append(cost) reg = max(reg / self.reg_factor, self.reg_min) if improvement < self.cost_tol: converged = True break return ILQRResult(X, U, cost_history, converged, it, reg)

This mirrors how every other deliverable in this series started with the smallest self-contained slice rather than the whole subsystem at once. Implementation choices worth being explicit about: - The neural network is implemented FROM SCRATCH in plain NumPy (no PyTorch/JAX/autodiff framework), for the same reason the rest of this series avoids heavy ML frameworks: it keeps the entire computation small enough to read start to finish and to validate every derivative against finite differences directly, the same rigor applied to the Gaussian risk fields and unicycle dynamics in the stochastic-iLQR deliverable. - The "ground truth" used to generate synthetic training data is ITSELF synthetic: the classical Pacejka formula plus a documented, hand-specified near-limit deviation (see `near_limit_deviation_force`) meant to resemble a real phenomenon (accelerated grip loss past the handling limit, e.g. from thermal/tread effects) that an idealized formula would miss. This is not real tire test-rig data, and the deliverable does not claim it is -- the point is to validate that the HYBRID MODELING APPROACH AND THE MATH work correctly, the same way the stochastic-iLQR deliverable validated risk-averse routing against synthetic Gaussian bumps rather than real perception output. """ from __future__ import annotations import math from dataclasses import dataclass, field from typing import List, Optional, Sequence, Tuple import numpy as np # ========================================================================== # Classical Pacejka ("Magic Formula") lateral tire model # ========================================================================== @dataclass(frozen=True) class PacejkaParams: """Standard simplified "Magic Formula" coefficients for LATERAL force vs. slip angle, plus a load-sensitivity term for the peak factor. Values are representative of a passenger-car tire (documented, not fit to any specific real vehicle): B : stiffness factor C : shape factor (~1.3 is a common literature value for lateral force) E : curvature factor (negative values produce the "falling" shape past the peak seen in real tire data) mu0: nominal peak friction coefficient at the reference load Fz0: reference normal load (N) at which mu0 applies load_sensitivity: fractional reduction in EFFECTIVE friction coefficient per unit (Fz - Fz0)/Fz0 -- models the real, well-known phenomenon that a tire's peak achievable friction COEFFICIENT (force/load) decreases somewhat as load increases, even though peak FORCE still increases. """ B: float = 8.0 C: float = 1.3 E: float = -1.0 mu0: float = 1.0 Fz0: float = 4000.0 load_sensitivity: float = 0.15 mu_floor: float = 0.05 # never let effective friction go to zero/negative def __post_init__(self): if self.Fz0 <= 0: raise ValueError("Fz0 must be > 0") if self.mu0 <= 0: raise ValueError("mu0 must be > 0") def peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Returns (D, dD/dFz) where D = mu_eff(Fz) * Fz is the Pacejka "peak factor" -- the maximum achievable lateral force at this load. mu_eff decreases linearly with load above Fz0 (load sensitivity), floored at `mu_floor`. """ Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) mu_eff_unclipped = params.mu0 * (1.0 - params.load_sensitivity * (Fz - params.Fz0) / params.Fz0) clipped = mu_eff_unclipped < params.mu_floor mu_eff = np.where(clipped, params.mu_floor, mu_eff_unclipped) d_mu_dFz = -params.mu0 * params.load_sensitivity / params.Fz0 D = mu_eff * Fz # Product rule EXCEPT where clipped, where mu_eff is locally # constant (the floor) and only the Fz factor contributes. dD_dFz = np.where(clipped, params.mu_floor, d_mu_dFz * Fz + mu_eff) return D, dD_dFz def lateral_force_classical(alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> np.ndarray: """Classical simplified Magic Formula: F_y = D * sin(C * arctan(B*alpha - E*(B*alpha - arctan(B*alpha)))) """ alpha = np.asarray(alpha, dtype=np.float64) Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) if np.any(Fz < 0): raise ValueError("normal load Fz must be >= 0") D, _ = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) Bx = params.B * alpha u = Bx - params.E * (Bx - np.arctan(Bx)) return D * np.sin(params.C * u) def lateral_force_classical_grad( alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams ) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Returns (F_y, dF_y/dalpha, dF_y/dFz) in closed form. u = B*alpha - E*(B*alpha - arctan(B*alpha)) du/dalpha = B*[(1-E) + E/(1+(B*alpha)^2)] F_y = D * sin(C*u) dF_y/dalpha = D * C * cos(C*u) * du/dalpha dF_y/dFz = (dD/dFz) * sin(C*u) [u does not depend on Fz] """ alpha = np.asarray(alpha, dtype=np.float64) Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) if np.any(Fz < 0): raise ValueError("normal load Fz must be >= 0") D, dD_dFz = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) Bx = params.B * alpha u = Bx - params.E * (Bx - np.arctan(Bx)) du_dalpha = params.B * ((1.0 - params.E) + params.E / (1.0 + Bx ** 2)) F_y = D * np.sin(params.C * u) dF_dalpha = D * params.C * np.cos(params.C * u) * du_dalpha dF_dFz = dD_dFz * np.sin(params.C * u) return F_y, dF_dalpha, dF_dFz def linear_cornering_stiffness(Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> np.ndarray: """Analytic small-slip-angle cornering stiffness dF_y/dalpha at alpha=0. A standard Pacejka identity: at alpha=0, u=0 so cos(C*u)=1 and du/dalpha = B*(1-E) + E*B = B, giving dF_y/dalpha|_{alpha=0} = D * C * B. """ D, _ = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) return D * params.C * params.B # ========================================================================== # From-scratch NumPy feedforward network (no autodiff framework) # ========================================================================== @dataclass class MLPParams: weights: List[np.ndarray] biases: List[np.ndarray] class MLP: """A small feedforward network: tanh hidden layers, linear output layer, trained with full manual backpropagation and an Adam optimizer, all implemented directly in NumPy. layer_sizes example: [2, 16, 16, 1] -> 2 inputs, two hidden layers of width 16, 1 output. """ def __init__(self, layer_sizes: Sequence[int], seed: int = 0): if len(layer_sizes) < 2: raise ValueError("layer_sizes must have at least an input and output layer") rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) weights = [] biases = [] for fan_in, fan_out in zip(layer_sizes[:-1], layer_sizes[1:]): # Xavier/Glorot initialization, standard for tanh networks. limit = math.sqrt(6.0 / (fan_in + fan_out)) weights.append(rng.uniform(-limit, limit, size=(fan_in, fan_out))) biases.append(np.zeros(fan_out)) self.params = MLPParams(weights=weights, biases=biases) self.layer_sizes = list(layer_sizes) @property def n_layers(self) -> int: return len(self.params.weights) # -- forward ----------------------------------------------------------- def forward(self, X: np.ndarray) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, dict]: """X: (N, in_dim). Returns (output (N, out_dim), cache) where cache holds every layer's pre-activation and activation, needed for backprop. """ X = np.atleast_2d(X) A = X cache = {"A": [A], "Z": []} n = self.n_layers for i in range(n): Z = A @ self.params.weights[i] + self.params.biases[i] cache["Z"].append(Z) if i < n - 1: A = np.tanh(Z) else: A = Z # linear output layer (regression) cache["A"].append(A) return A, cache def predict(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: out, _ = self.forward(X) return out # -- backward ------------------------------------------------------------ def backward(self, cache: dict, dL_dout: np.ndarray) -> Tuple[List[np.ndarray], List[np.ndarray], np.ndarray]: """Standard backprop. Returns (dW list, db list, dL/dInput). `dL_dout` is the gradient of whatever scalar/vector quantity the caller cares about, w.r.t. the network's OUTPUT -- for training this is dLoss/dOutput; for computing the network's own input-Jacobian (used by the hybrid tire model's analytic gradient), this is simply an all-ones vector (see `input_gradient` below). """ n = self.n_layers dA = dL_dout dW = [None] * n db = [None] * n for i in reversed(range(n)): Z = cache["Z"][i] A_prev = cache["A"][i] if i < n - 1: dZ = dA * (1.0 - np.tanh(Z) ** 2) else: dZ = dA # linear output layer dW[i] = A_prev.T @ dZ db[i] = dZ.sum(axis=0) dA = dZ @ self.params.weights[i].T dL_dinput = dA return dW, db, dL_dinput def input_gradient(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: """dOutput/dInput for a (N, in_dim) batch, assuming a scalar (1-dimensional) output -- exactly the case used by the hybrid tire model. Reuses the same backward() pass used for training, seeded with an all-ones output gradient instead of a loss gradient; this is legitimate because dOutput/dInput IS backward() with dL/dOutput = 1. """ out, cache = self.forward(X) if out.shape[1] != 1: raise ValueError("input_gradient only supports scalar-output networks") _, _, dL_dinput = self.backward(cache, np.ones_like(out)) return dL_dinput # -- training -------------------------------------------------------- def train( self, X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray, epochs: int = 2000, lr: float = 0.01, batch_size: Optional[int] = None, seed: int = 0, ) -> List[float]: """Full manual Adam optimizer, MSE loss. Returns the per-epoch loss history (on the full training set, regardless of batch_size) for convergence checking. """ X = np.atleast_2d(X) y = np.asarray(y, dtype=np.float64).reshape(-1, 1) N = X.shape[0] rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) beta1, beta2, eps = 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8 m_w = [np.zeros_like(w) for w in self.params.weights] v_w = [np.zeros_like(w) for w in self.params.weights] m_b = [np.zeros_like(b) for b in self.params.biases] v_b = [np.zeros_like(b) for b in self.params.biases] t = 0 loss_history = [] bs = batch_size or N for epoch in range(epochs): perm = rng.permutation(N) for start in range(0, N, bs): idx = perm[start : start + bs] Xb, yb = X[idx], y[idx] out, cache = self.forward(Xb) dL_dout = 2.0 * (out - yb) / Xb.shape[0] # d(MSE)/d(out) dW, db, _ = self.backward(cache, dL_dout) t += 1 for i in range(self.n_layers): m_w[i] = beta1 * m_w[i] + (1 - beta1) * dW[i] v_w[i] = beta2 * v_w[i] + (1 - beta2) * dW[i] ** 2 m_hat = m_w[i] / (1 - beta1 ** t) v_hat = v_w[i] / (1 - beta2 ** t) self.params.weights[i] -= lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps) m_b[i] = beta1 * m_b[i] + (1 - beta1) * db[i] v_b[i] = beta2 * v_b[i] + (1 - beta2) * db[i] ** 2 m_hat_b = m_b[i] / (1 - beta1 ** t) v_hat_b = v_b[i] / (1 - beta2 ** t) self.params.biases[i] -= lr * m_hat_b / (np.sqrt(v_hat_b) + eps) full_out = self.predict(X) loss_history.append(float(np.mean((full_out - y) ** 2))) return loss_history # ========================================================================== # Synthetic "ground truth" (near-limit deviation the classical model misses) # ========================================================================== def near_limit_deviation_force(alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> np.ndarray: """A documented, hand-specified systematic deviation representing accelerated grip loss past a slip-angle threshold -- the kind of thermal/tread-wear effect a real tire exhibits near its handling limit that an idealized Magic Formula, fit primarily to moderate-slip data, tends to miss. This is SYNTHETIC by construction (see module docstring) -- it exists to give the residual network something structured and physically-motivated to learn, not to model any specific real tire. Shape: zero below the threshold, ramping smoothly (not a hard corner) to up to 15% of the classical peak force beyond it, always acting to REDUCE the force magnitude (i.e. opposing alpha's sign), consistent with "the tire has less grip than the idealized formula predicts, once you're far enough into the slip range." """ alpha = np.asarray(alpha, dtype=np.float64) Fz = np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64) threshold = 0.12 # rad, ~6.9 deg -- near where lateral force typically peaks max_fraction = 0.15 ramp_rate = 15.0 D, _ = peak_factor_and_derivative(Fz, params) excess = np.clip(np.abs(alpha) - threshold, 0.0, None) shape = 1.0 - np.exp(-ramp_rate * excess) magnitude = max_fraction * D * shape return -np.sign(alpha) * magnitude def ground_truth_lateral_force(alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray, params: PacejkaParams) -> np.ndarray: return lateral_force_classical(alpha, Fz, params) + near_limit_deviation_force(alpha, Fz, params) def generate_synthetic_dataset( n_samples: int, params: PacejkaParams, alpha_range: Tuple[float, float] = (-0.35, 0.35), Fz_range: Tuple[float, float] = (2000.0, 8000.0), noise_std_n: float = 15.0, seed: int = 0, ) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Generates (alpha, Fz, noisy_ground_truth_force) triples, simulating what a real tire test rig's raw measurements might look like: samples across the slip-angle/load operating range, ground truth from `ground_truth_lateral_force`, plus i.i.d. Gaussian measurement noise (`noise_std_n` Newtons). """ rng = np.random.default_rng(seed) alpha = rng.uniform(*alpha_range, size=n_samples) Fz = rng.uniform(*Fz_range, size=n_samples) true_force = ground_truth_lateral_force(alpha, Fz, params) noisy_force = true_force + rng.normal(0.0, noise_std_n, size=n_samples) return alpha, Fz, noisy_force # ========================================================================== # Hybrid model # ========================================================================== @dataclass class InputNormalization: alpha_scale: float Fz_scale: float class NeuralPacejkaTireModel: """Classical Pacejka + a trained MLP residual, with analytic gradients combining the classical closed-form derivative and the MLP's backprop-based input gradient via the chain rule through input normalization. """ def __init__(self, params: PacejkaParams, mlp: MLP, normalization: InputNormalization): self.params = params self.mlp = mlp self.normalization = normalization def _normalized_inputs(self, alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: alpha = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(alpha, dtype=np.float64)) Fz = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(Fz, dtype=np.float64)) x1 = alpha / self.normalization.alpha_scale x2 = Fz / self.normalization.Fz_scale return np.stack([x1, x2], axis=1) def force(self, alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray: classical = lateral_force_classical(alpha, Fz, self.params) X = self._normalized_inputs(alpha, Fz) residual = self.mlp.predict(X).reshape(-1) result = classical.reshape(-1) + residual if np.ndim(classical) else classical + residual return result if np.ndim(np.asarray(alpha)) else float(result[0]) def force_and_grad( self, alpha: np.ndarray, Fz: np.ndarray ) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Returns (F_y, dF_y/dalpha, dF_y/dFz), combining: - the classical model's closed-form derivative, plus - the MLP residual's derivative via the chain rule: d(residual)/dalpha = d(residual)/d(x1) * dx1/dalpha = mlp_input_grad[:,0] / alpha_scale d(residual)/dFz = mlp_input_grad[:,1] / Fz_scale """ scalar_input = np.ndim(np.asarray(alpha)) == 0 F_classical, dF_dalpha_classical, dF_dFz_classical = lateral_force_classical_grad( alpha, Fz, self.params ) X = self._normalized_inputs(alpha, Fz) residual = self.mlp.predict(X).reshape(-1) input_grad = self.mlp.input_gradient(X) # (N, 2) dresidual_dalpha = input_grad[:, 0] / self.normalization.alpha_scale dresidual_dFz = input_grad[:, 1] / self.normalization.Fz_scale F_total = np.atleast_1d(F_classical) + residual dF_dalpha = np.atleast_1d(dF_dalpha_classical) + dresidual_dalpha dF_dFz = np.atleast_1d(dF_dFz_classical) + dresidual_dFz if scalar_input: return float(F_total[0]), float(dF_dalpha[0]), float(dF_dFz[0]) return F_total, dF_dalpha, dF_dFz def train_neural_pacejka( params: PacejkaParams, alpha_train: np.ndarray, Fz_train: np.ndarray, residual_target: np.ndarray, hidden_sizes: Sequence[int] = (16, 16), epochs: int = 6000, lr: float = 0.02, seed: int = 0, normalization: Optional[InputNormalization] = None, ) -> Tuple[NeuralPacejkaTireModel, List[float]]: """Convenience constructor: builds and trains the residual MLP on (alpha, Fz) -> residual_target, and returns the assembled hybrid model plus the training loss history. """ normalization = normalization or InputNormalization(alpha_scale=0.35, Fz_scale=8000.0) mlp = MLP([2, *hidden_sizes, 1], seed=seed) x1 = np.asarray(alpha_train) / normalization.alpha_scale x2 = np.asarray(Fz_train) / normalization.Fz_scale X = np.stack([x1, x2], axis=1) loss_history = mlp.train(X, residual_target, epochs=epochs, lr=lr, seed=seed) return NeuralPacejkaTireModel(params, mlp, normalization), loss_history

""" bicycle_dynamics_tire_model.py ---------------------------------- Wires the neural-Pacejka hybrid tire model (previous deliverable) into an actual DYNAMIC bicycle model -- replacing the kinematic unicycle used everywhere else in this series with a vehicle model that has real mass, yaw inertia, load transfer, and tire forces that genuinely saturate. This closes the gap flagged at the end of the tire-model deliverable: "Fz here is still an independent input, not a real state-dependent quantity." State (6-dim): s = [x, y, heading, vx, vy, r] x, y : position (m), world frame heading : yaw angle (rad) vx, vy : body-frame longitudinal / lateral velocity (m/s) r : yaw rate (rad/s) Control (2-dim): u = [delta, Fx] delta : front steer angle (rad) Fx : longitudinal drive/brake force (N), applied at the rear (see "Scope" below) Scope, deliberately narrow (consistent with every other deliverable in this series): - Single-track (bicycle) model: front and rear axles each lumped into one tire. - NO combined slip: the front tire carries lateral force only (no front-wheel drive/brake force feeding into a friction-ellipse coupling with its lateral slip) -- this mirrors the neural-Pacejka tire model's OWN declared scope (lateral force only), so nothing here asks that model a question it wasn't built to answer. - Longitudinal load transfer only (no lateral/roll load transfer, i.e. no roll dynamics) -- Fz for each axle depends on the commanded longitudinal force, using a static approximation (see `axle_normal_loads`) that avoids an implicit fixed-point between Fz and the tire forces it partly determines. - The iLQR solver included here is a MINIMAL, generic (dynamics-pluggable) reimplementation, with a simple goal + control-effort cost -- it deliberately does NOT reintroduce the Gaussian-bump risk-field machinery from the stochastic-iLQR deliverable. Combining risk-aware routing with real tire dynamics is a natural, substantial follow-up, not attempted here. Every analytic derivative in this file -- slip angles, load transfer, the full continuous-time dynamics Jacobian (which chains through the tire model's OWN already-validated gradient) -- is checked against finite differences in the test suite, the same standard applied to every differentiable component built earlier in this series. """ from __future__ import annotations import math from dataclasses import dataclass from typing import Callable, List, Optional, Tuple import numpy as np from neural_pacejka import NeuralPacejkaTireModel STATE_DIM = 6 CONTROL_DIM = 2 # ========================================================================== # Vehicle parameters # ========================================================================== @dataclass(frozen=True) class VehicleParams: """Representative passenger-car parameters (documented, not fit to any specific real vehicle).""" mass_kg: float = 1500.0 yaw_inertia_kgm2: float = 2500.0 lf_m: float = 1.2 # CG to front axle lr_m: float = 1.6 # CG to rear axle cg_height_m: float = 0.5 gravity_mps2: float = 9.81 vx_floor_mps: float = 0.5 # slip-angle denominator floor, see module docstring fz_floor_n: float = 200.0 # never let a computed normal load reach zero/negative def __post_init__(self): if self.mass_kg <= 0 or self.yaw_inertia_kgm2 <= 0: raise ValueError("mass and yaw inertia must be > 0") if self.lf_m <= 0 or self.lr_m <= 0: raise ValueError("lf_m and lr_m must be > 0") @property def wheelbase_m(self) -> float: return self.lf_m + self.lr_m # ========================================================================== # Axle normal loads (longitudinal load transfer only) # ========================================================================== def axle_normal_loads(Fx: float, vp: VehicleParams) -> Tuple[float, float]: """Static + longitudinal-load-transfer normal loads. Fzf_static = m*g*lr / L, Fzr_static = m*g*lf / L Fzf = Fzf_static - Fx*h/L, Fzr = Fzr_static + Fx*h/L using ax_approx = Fx/m for the load-transfer term (ignoring the tire forces' own, much smaller, contribution to longitudinal acceleration for this purpose -- see module docstring). This keeps Fz an explicit, non-implicit function of the CONTROL alone, while still making it a genuine, physically-motivated function of the vehicle's commanded behavior rather than a fixed constant. """ L = vp.wheelbase_m Fzf_static = vp.mass_kg * vp.gravity_mps2 * vp.lr_m / L Fzr_static = vp.mass_kg * vp.gravity_mps2 * vp.lf_m / L transfer = Fx * vp.cg_height_m / L Fzf = max(Fzf_static - transfer, vp.fz_floor_n) Fzr = max(Fzr_static + transfer, vp.fz_floor_n) return Fzf, Fzr def axle_normal_loads_grad(Fx: float, vp: VehicleParams) -> Tuple[float, float, float, float]: """Returns (Fzf, dFzf/dFx, Fzr, dFzr/dFx).""" L = vp.wheelbase_m Fzf_static = vp.mass_kg * vp.gravity_mps2 * vp.lr_m / L Fzr_static = vp.mass_kg * vp.gravity_mps2 * vp.lf_m / L transfer = Fx * vp.cg_height_m / L Fzf_raw = Fzf_static - transfer Fzr_raw = Fzr_static + transfer dFzf_dFx = -vp.cg_height_m / L if Fzf_raw > vp.fz_floor_n else 0.0 dFzr_dFx = vp.cg_height_m / L if Fzr_raw > vp.fz_floor_n else 0.0 Fzf = max(Fzf_raw, vp.fz_floor_n) Fzr = max(Fzr_raw, vp.fz_floor_n) return Fzf, dFzf_dFx, Fzr, dFzr_dFx # ========================================================================== # Slip angles # ========================================================================== def slip_angles(state: np.ndarray, delta: float, vp: VehicleParams) -> Tuple[float, float]: """alpha_f = delta - atan2(vy + lf*r, vx_eff) alpha_r = -atan2(vy - lr*r, vx_eff) with vx_eff = max(vx, vx_floor) guarding the near-zero-speed singularity in the slip-angle definition (a standard practical treatment; see module docstring). """ _, _, _, vx, vy, r = state vx_eff = max(vx, vp.vx_floor_mps) alpha_f = delta - math.atan2(vy + vp.lf_m * r, vx_eff) alpha_r = -math.atan2(vy - vp.lr_m * r, vx_eff) return alpha_f, alpha_r def slip_angles_grad( state: np.ndarray, delta: float, vp: VehicleParams ) -> Tuple[float, float, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]: """Returns (alpha_f, alpha_r, d(alpha_f)/d[vx,vy,r,delta], d(alpha_r)/d[vx,vy,r,delta]) using atan2's standard partials (d/dy atan2(y,x) = x/(x^2+y^2), d/dx atan2(y,x) = -y/(x^2+y^2)). The vx_eff floor's derivative is 0 when clipped (a flat region), consistent with the same floor-clipping treatment used elsewhere in this series (the tire model's own mu floor, the Simplex Guard's friction floor). """ _, _, _, vx, vy, r = state clipped = vx <= vp.vx_floor_mps vx_eff = vp.vx_floor_mps if clipped else vx dvx_eff_dvx = 0.0 if clipped else 1.0 yf = vy + vp.lf_m * r denom_f = yf ** 2 + vx_eff ** 2 dalphaf_dvy = -vx_eff / denom_f dalphaf_dr = -vp.lf_m * vx_eff / denom_f dalphaf_dvx = (yf / denom_f) * dvx_eff_dvx dalphaf_ddelta = 1.0 yr = vy - combined_slip_tire_model.py

""" combined_slip_tire_model.py ------------------------------ Closes the scope gap flagged at the end of the bicycle-dynamics deliverable: the front tire there carried lateral force only (no coupling to braking/driving force), which was a deliberate simplification matching the neural-Pacejka tire model's own declared scope. This adds a FRICTION-CIRCLE combined-slip coupling on BOTH axles, and extends the bicycle model's control from [delta, Fx] (one longitudinal force, applied entirely at the rear) to [delta, Fxf, Fxr] (independently commandable front and rear longitudinal force) -- so there is now something for the front tire's lateral capacity to actually be coupled against. Physics: a tire's total available grip at a given normal load is bounded (the classical Pacejka peak factor D). Using SOME of that capacity for longitudinal force (braking/driving) leaves LESS available for lateral force, and vice versa -- the real reason a car understeers more when trail-braking into a corner, or why braking hard mid-turn can make a front tire "give up" its cornering grip. This is modeled here with the standard, simplified FRICTION CIRCLE (not ellipse -- i.e. using the SAME peak magnitude D for both directions, rather than separate longitudinal/lateral peak coefficients, since the neural-Pacejka tire model was built and validated for lateral force only and does not have an independently validated longitudinal peak to draw an ellipse with -- documented scope decision, not an oversight): scaling(Fx_tire, D) = sqrt(max(0, 1 - (Fx_tire/D)^2)) F_y_combined = F_y_pure_lateral(alpha, Fz) * scaling(Fx_tire, D) where D = D(Fz) is the CLASSICAL Pacejka peak factor (not the trained hybrid model's own, not independently characterized, near-limit behavior) -- using the analytically known classical peak as the friction-circle radius is itself a standard simplification in combined-slip literature, and is a cleaner, more principled choice here than trying to extract a "peak" from a black-box residual that was never trained to expose one. Nothing in neural_pacejka.py or bicycle_dynamics_tire_model.py is modified -- `axle_normal_loads`/`_grad` and `slip_angles`/`_grad` are reused UNCHANGED (load transfer depends on TOTAL longitudinal force, regardless of how it's split between axles, so nothing about those functions needs to change), and `GenericILQRSolver` is reused UNCHANGED too (it was already generalized to accept any state/control dimension via pluggable functions in the previous deliverable, specifically so this kind of extension wouldn't require touching it). """ from __future__ import annotations import math from typing import Tuple import numpy as np from neural_pacejka import NeuralPacejkaTireModel, PacejkaParams, peak_factor_and_derivative from bicycle_dynamics_tire_model import ( VehicleParams, axle_normal_loads, axle_normal_loads_grad, slip_angles, slip_angles_grad, ) STATE_DIM = 6 CONTROL_DIM = 3 # [delta, Fxf, Fxr] SCALING_FLOOR = 0.05 # never let combined-slip lateral capacity reach exactly zero RATIO_CLIP = 0.999 # keep Fx/D strictly inside the friction circle's smooth interior # ========================================================================== # Friction-circle combined-slip scaling # ========================================================================== def combined_slip_scaling(Fx_tire: float, D: float) -> float: """scaling = sqrt(max(0, 1 - (Fx_tire/D)^2)), clipped/floored for numerical safety at the friction-circle boundary (same treatment as every other physical floor in this series: a flat, zero- derivative region rather than an undefined one).""" ratio = Fx_tire / D ratio_c = max(-RATIO_CLIP, min(RATIO_CLIP, ratio)) scaling = math.sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - ratio_c ** 2)) return max(scaling, SCALING_FLOOR) def combined_slip_scaling_grad(Fx_tire: float, D: float, dD_dFz: float) -> Tuple[float, float, float]: """Returns (scaling, d(scaling)/d(Fx_tire), d(scaling)/dFz) -- the Fz-derivative chains through the given dD/dFz (from `peak_factor_and_derivative`). """ ratio = Fx_tire / D clipped = abs(ratio) >= RATIO_CLIP ratio_c = max(-RATIO_CLIP, min(RATIO_CLIP, ratio)) scaling_raw = math.sqrt(max(0.0, 1.0 - ratio_c ** 2)) floored = scaling_raw <= SCALING_FLOOR scaling = max(scaling_raw, SCALING_FLOOR) if clipped or floored or scaling_raw <= 1e-9: return scaling, 0.0, 0.0 d_scaling_d_ratio = -ratio_c / scaling_raw d_ratio_d_Fx = 1.0 / D d_scaling_d_Fz def combined_slip_lateral_force( alpha: float, Fz: float, Fx_tire: float, tire_model: NeuralPacejkaTireModel, pacejka_params: PacejkaParams ) -> float: F_y_pure = 10. What this is, and isn't

This validates that risk-averse routing composes correctly with real vehicle dynamics, and surfaces a genuine, previously-unconfirmed fact about the symmetric-saddle limitation along the way. It is explicitly not:

A demonstration that risk-aware routing and grip saturation actively interact in an interesting way. The honest result here is that the monotonic-clearance property holds up essentially unchanged across the speed range tested — the qualitative behavior is the same at 10, 20, and 30 m/s, once the scenario is properly scaled to each speed's travel distance. A more targeted experiment — e.g., an obstacle placed such that avoiding it requires steering hard enough to approach the tire's saturation point at high speed specifically, forcing a genuine trade-off between detour aggressiveness and grip margin — might find a more dramatic interaction. This deliverable did not go looking for that scenario specifically; it answered the more basic question (does it still work at all) first.

Combined with the trail-braking finding from the previous deliverable. The headline scenario here uses steering-only avoidance (no braking commanded by the optimizer in any of the reported runs) — whether a risk-averse optimizer would ever choose to trade some braking-induced grip loss for a tighter avoidance maneuver, or vice versa, is an open question this deliverable's cost weights didn't specifically probe.

A resolution of the finite-horizon planning effect described in Section 4. The lowest-speed, highest-risk-weight case's larger residual offset is measured and explained, not eliminated.

This closes the last explicitly-open item from the combined-slip deliverable's write-up. What remains open across the series: the phantom-force/asymmetry limitation in the trained tire model (flagged in the bicycle-dynamics and combined-slip deliverables, still unaddressed), and the two more targeted follow-up experiments named just above — a scenario specifically designed to force a detour/grip trade-off, and a risk-aware scenario where braking is genuinely competitive with steering as an avoidance strategy.
